Most claim validation systems fail in the same boring way: they look great on the demo set, then quietly drift on the long tail. The fix isn't a better prompt. It's a golden set you trust, evals that pressure-test your policy assumptions, and review artifacts that trace every decision back to the specific claim it applies to.

## The practical problem

When a system says "this claim is supported" or "this needs review," someone downstream has to act on that label. If you can't reconstruct *why* the system reached that verdict — which claim, against which evidence, under which policy — you're not running a validation pipeline. You're running a vibes engine with logs.

This shows up most painfully when:

- A reviewer disagrees with a verdict and there's no clean way to localize the disagreement.
- A policy changes and you can't tell which past decisions would flip.
- A model update ships and regressions only surface in production.

## Golden sets are the substrate, not the decoration

A golden set is the artifact that makes everything else honest. It should encode:

- **Claims at the right grain.** One sentence with three claims is three rows, not one. If your set treats it as one, your evals will quietly average over disagreements you can't see.
- **Evidence linkage.** Each claim points to the specific supporting (or contradicting) passages, with span-level references where possible.
- **Adjudicated labels.** Not "model A said X." A human-resolved verdict with a short rationale. The rationale is what lets you re-adjudicate when the policy shifts.
- **Hard cases on purpose.** Ambiguous scope, partial support, stale evidence, and near-miss paraphrases. If your golden set is all easy calls, your eval is theater.

The useful split: a stable core set that defines correctness, plus a rotating challenge set that captures new failure modes as you find them. The core set rarely changes. The challenge set is where learning happens.

## Where naive evals break

A single accuracy number against a balanced set will tell you almost nothing useful. The failure modes that matter live in slices:

- **Policy-sensitive slices.** Claims that hinge on a specific definition (what counts as "current," what counts as "supported by the source"). These are where a policy rewrite silently changes outcomes.
- **Evidence-quality slices.** Strong evidence vs. weak vs. absent. Systems that look fine overall often collapse when evidence is thin and they have to abstain instead of guess.
- **Paraphrase and entailment slices.** Lexical overlap is not support. If your eval doesn't separate these, you're rewarding the wrong behavior.

Measurement first: decide which slices you'd refuse to ship a regression on, and gate releases on those — not on the aggregate.

## Claim-level traceability in review artifacts

Reviewers need to operate on the same units the system does. That means the review UI and the stored artifact should both expose:

- The extracted claim, verbatim.
- The evidence the system considered, with what it used and what it ignored.
- The policy clause invoked (e.g., recency threshold, source-type rule).
- The verdict and a machine-readable reason code, not just prose.

When all four are persisted per claim, three useful things become possible. You can re-run evals against past decisions when the policy changes. You can compute inter-rater agreement at the claim level instead of the document level. And you can give the model targeted feedback on the specific step that went wrong — extraction, retrieval, or judgment — instead of blaming "the model."

## The review loop that actually moves the needle

The loop we'd recommend building toward:

1. **Sample from production**, stratified by slice and by model confidence. Don't just review what looks wrong.
2. **Adjudicate at the claim level** with a short rationale and a reason code from a controlled vocabulary.
3. **Promote disagreements** into the challenge set. Disagreements between reviewers are more informative than disagreements between model and reviewer.
4. **Re-run the full eval** before any policy or model change ships. Diff the verdicts, not just the metrics.
5. **Track drift** by re-scoring a fixed production sample weekly against the current system. Aggregate metrics hide drift; fixed samples expose it.

## What to copy

If you're building claim validation and want something concrete to take away:

- Start the golden set before the model. The act of writing it forces the policy to become explicit.
- Store claims, evidence spans, policy clauses, and reason codes as first-class fields in every artifact.
- Gate releases on slice-level regressions, not aggregate accuracy.
- Treat the challenge set as a living document owned by whoever reviews production output.

None of this is glamorous. It's the real design work that separates a claim validator you'd stake a decision on from one that just produces confident-sounding labels.
