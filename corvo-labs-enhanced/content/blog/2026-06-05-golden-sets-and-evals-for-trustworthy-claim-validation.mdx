Claim validation systems fail in a specific, predictable way: they look great in demos and fall apart the first time a reviewer asks "why did it pass this one?" The fix isn't a better prompt. It's a golden set, a real eval loop, and review artifacts that trace back to individual claims.

This post walks through how we think about that stack at Corvo Labs, using FreshProof-style claim validation as the working example.

## The practical problem

A claim validation system takes a piece of content — a marketing page, a label, a clinical summary — extracts the factual claims, and checks each one against policy, evidence, or both. The naive version is a single LLM call: "here is the content, here is the policy, tell me what's wrong."

That version passes the first ten examples you try. Then a reviewer flags a borderline claim, you tweak the prompt, and three previously-passing examples silently regress. There's no way to know unless you measure.

## Golden sets are the unglamorous foundation

A golden set is a curated collection of inputs with known, human-adjudicated expected outputs at the claim level. Not document-level. Claim-level.

The real design work is in the curation:

- **Coverage of policy edge cases**, not just the obvious violations. The interesting examples are the ones where two reviewers might disagree on first read.
- **Negative examples that look positive**, and vice versa. A claim that uses hedged language but is still non-compliant. A claim that sounds aggressive but is fully substantiated.
- **Stable labels with rationale**. Each expected judgment needs a short written reason, so when the model disagrees you can tell whether the model is wrong or the label was sloppy.

Golden sets are small on purpose. A few hundred well-adjudicated claims beats ten thousand auto-labeled ones, because you'll actually trust the eval results.

## What evals expose that prompts hide

Once you have a golden set, you run the system against it on every meaningful change. The interesting output isn't the aggregate pass rate — it's the diffs.

A few patterns we see repeatedly:

- **Brittle policy assumptions.** The system implicitly assumes claims are written in a particular voice or structure. Reword the same claim and the judgment flips. Evals catch this because the golden set includes paraphrases.
- **Category collapse.** The model handles 80% of claim types well and one category badly, but the aggregate metric hides it. Slice the eval by claim type and the failure becomes obvious.
- **Policy drift.** Someone updates the policy doc, the prompt picks up new language, and old decisions silently change. Without a golden set you find out from a reviewer; with one you find out in CI.

The useful split is between **system evals** (does the pipeline behave correctly on known inputs) and **policy evals** (does the policy itself produce the judgments humans actually want). The second one is harder and more important.

## Claim-level traceability in review artifacts

The other half of trustworthy validation is what the reviewer sees. If the output is a document-level verdict with a paragraph of reasoning, reviewers can't efficiently agree or disagree. They have to re-read everything.

Review artifacts should be structured per claim:

- The extracted claim, verbatim.
- The specific policy rule or evidence it was checked against.
- The judgment and a short rationale.
- A pointer back to the source span in the original content.

This does two things. It makes review fast, because a reviewer can scan and accept or override claim by claim. And it produces structured feedback data — every override is a labeled example that can flow back into the golden set.

That feedback loop is what turns a static eval into a system that improves over time without anyone hand-writing new test cases.

## What to copy

If you're building anything in this shape, the minimum viable version is:

1. A golden set of 100–300 claims with claim-level expected judgments and written rationale.
2. An eval harness that runs on every prompt, policy, or model change and reports diffs, not just aggregates.
3. Review artifacts that decompose to the claim level and capture overrides as structured data.
4. A weekly or monthly loop where overrides are triaged and the strongest ones graduate into the golden set.

None of this is exotic. It's measurement first, applied to a problem where the alternative is hoping the prompt still works. For claim validation specifically, that hope is not a strategy — reviewers will notice, and so will whoever is downstream of the decisions.
